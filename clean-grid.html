Canada's Clean Grid Has a Rainy-Day Problem

A power system built around hydropower looks clean on paper. In a dry year, it leans on the same fossil fuels it was meant to replace — and the bill comes due in carbon, not just dollars.

By Hridhaye Bir Singh October 14, 2025 8 min read

Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit. Canada has, for most of its modern history, told itself a simple story about electricity: the lights run on water. It is a story with real evidence behind it. Roughly three out of every five kilowatt-hours produced in this country come from hydropower, a share unrivalled in the G7. The country's climate plans, its export pitch, and a good deal of its self-image rest on that single statistic.

What the statistic does not capture is the weather. In a wet year, the math is generous and the grid behaves the way the brochures promise. In a dry one, the reservoirs draw down faster than they refill, and the system has to find its missing electrons somewhere. Often, that somewhere is on the other side of a fossil-fuel turbine.

The drought that exposed the seam

British Columbia spent much of 2023 and the first half of 2024 importing power. That was unusual, but it was not mysterious. The province had endured back-to-back dry years, BC Hydro's reservoirs were sitting well below their long-term averages, and the cheapest available megawatts were sitting in Washington, Alberta, and points further south — many of them produced by natural gas. The grid, in other words, did exactly what it was designed to do. It kept the lights on. It just did so at a higher carbon cost than the public storyline allowed for.

The point

"A grid built around water is a grid that, in a dry year, quietly burns gas on someone else's balance sheet."

This is the part of the conversation that tends to get lost. Hydropower is not a fossil fuel, and the question is not whether to keep building it. The question is what a system optimized around a weather-dependent baseload does when the weather stops cooperating, and how honestly we account for the answer.

What the numbers actually show

Look at the import data for the past two drought years and a pattern emerges. The emissions intensity of BC's consumed electricity — the carbon attached to the power British Columbians actually used, not the power their dams produced — moved noticeably upward in dry quarters. That is a number nobody puts on a billboard.

None of this is an argument against hydropower. It is an argument for honesty about what a hydropower-heavy grid is and is not. It is firm capacity in a normal year. It is a variable resource in a hot, dry one. Pretending otherwise is how a country ends up importing emissions it has already claimed credit for avoiding.

What a serious answer looks like

A serious answer probably involves more interties, more firm low-carbon capacity, and a more candid public conversation about what "clean" means in a year when the rain doesn't come. None of that is glamorous. All of it is cheaper than the alternative, which is paying for the same kilowatt-hour twice — once in dollars, once in carbon — and pretending we didn't.
